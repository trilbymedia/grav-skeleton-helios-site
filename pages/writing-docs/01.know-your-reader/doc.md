Every page of documentation is read by someone in the middle of something. They were installing, configuring, debugging or deciding, they hit a question, and they went looking. The page succeeds if it answers that question quickly, and fails if it makes them read around it.

So before you write a word, work out who lands on the page and what they were doing a minute before.

## Three readers, three pages

Most docs serve three kinds of reader, and they want different things from the same topic:

- **The evaluator** is deciding whether to use the software at all. They want to know what it does, what it costs them and whether it fits. They skim.
- **The newcomer** has decided, and wants to get from nothing to something working. They follow steps in order and copy what you show them.
- **The regular** already uses it and needs one fact: an option name, a default, the right command. They search, land, find it and leave.

A page that tries to serve all three serves none of them well. The evaluator wades through setup steps, the newcomer gets a wall of options before the first working example, and the regular scrolls past both to find the table at the bottom.

> [!TIP]
> If you can't tell which reader a page is for, look at how people reach it. A page found from search is usually read by a regular. A page linked from your home page is read by an evaluator or a newcomer.

## Write down the question

Pick the page you brought to this course and write one sentence at the top of a scratch file: the question a reader has when they open it. Not the topic, the question.

| Topic | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Configuration | How do I change the port the server listens on? |
| Caching | Why do my changes not show up until I clear the cache? |
| Installation | What is the fastest way to get this running on my laptop? |

A topic gives you something to write about. A question gives you something to answer, and tells you when you are done.
